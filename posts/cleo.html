Cléo from 5 to 7

November 17, 2023
"I think my fear is gone. I think I’m happy."

Cléo from 5 to 7 is a French New Wave drama written and directed by Agnès Varda.

Synopsis: "Agnès Varda eloquently captures Paris in the sixties with this real-time portrait of a singer set adrift in the city as she awaits test results of a biopsy. A chronicle of the minutes of one woman’s life, Cléo from 5 to 7 is a spirited mix of vivid vérité and melodrama, featuring a score by Michel Legrand and cameos by Jean-Luc Godard and Anna Karina."

This was a wonderful watch. I've never seen a film follow a character over such a small and specific period of time. We follow Cléo over the course of two hours, 5:00 PM through 7:00 PM, as she anxiously awaits her cancer testing results.

There's a purposeful contrast between the lively streets surrounding Cléo and the looming thought of death. The camera actively participates in the film, moving independently to explore the streets of Paris, pushing into typically ignored details, and looking around at the crowd around Cléo as she examines herself in the mirror. The camera dances as it sways and pans back and forth in time with the rhythm of the music and the characters' movements. Varda's smooth tracking shots and long takes combined with handheld camera work help to ground the film in a realistic portrait of 1960s Paris.

The actual camera work plays a part in the theme and plot of this film as well. Early in the film, the camera and framing emphasize mirrors and reflections as Cléo is locked into a loop of self-absorption and how others view her. As the afternoon progresses and she faces her mortality, the camera's gaze opens up to the external world, strangers, and her eventual encounter with a soldier leaving soon for war. Mirrors initially serve as Cléo’s security blanket, validating her existence through her beauty ("As long as I'm beautiful, I'm alive"). Later, when she shatters a mirror or walks past them without looking, it visually shows her growth toward her more authentic self.

The film structurally splits into two halves. In the first half, Cléo is looked at by the world. In the second half, she steps out of her performative identity, puts on a simple black dress, and begins to look at the world around her. She takes off her wig at the exact midpoint, and by the end, we learn that her name isn't really Cléo (which is an even bigger wig, so to speak).

Cléo begins as self-concerned and anxious, but we watch her grow throughout these two hours. Her growth serves as a reflection of how our selfishness can be our own downfall. True freedom comes from becoming an active participant who observes and connects with the world.

★★★★★
